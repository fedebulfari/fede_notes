# 1. Photodiode

Two-terminal device that produces a current when stimulated by photons.

![[Light Sensor-1791980180271.webp|455x267]]

It is used with reverse bias (voltage at cathode > voltage at anode) in order to better distinguish the different generated currents.

![[Light Sensor-1791980682491.webp|455]]

## Current Generation

![[Light Sensor-1791980956918.webp]]

1. Electron/hole pairs generation (photo-generation + thermal generation)
2. Carriers are attracted by reverse bias
3. Carriers form a photoelectric current

There is a high probability of electron recombination in the P region. With reverse bias, electrons travel toward the cathode, therefore they have low probability to recombine.

## P-N vs P-I-N

![[Light Sensor-1791981591420.webp|555]]

P-I-N has an intrinsic layer between P and N, this means the depletion region will be thicker. This means:
- higher sensitivity / responsivity in a wider spectrum
- reduced capacitance (that usually introduces problems with instability of the circuit)

## Penetration Depth

**Penetration depth**: depth at which the intensity of the radiation inside the material falls to $\frac{1}{e}$ (about 37%) of its original value at the surface.

![[Light Sensor-1791981848603.webp|383]]

Photo-generation requires: energy of photons must be higher than the energy gap of silicon: $E_{ph} > E_g$ (Si: $E_g = 1.12 \, eV$).

## Responsivity

![[Light Sensor-1791984039609.webp|375]]

**Photodiode Responsivity (sensitivity)**: ratio of the photocurrent $I_{ph}$ to the incident light power $P$ at a given wavelength: $$R_{\lambda}=\frac{I_{ph}}{P}$$

$R_{\lambda}$ depends on:
- incident light wavelength
- applied $V_{rev}$
- temperature

![[Light Sensor-1791984216910.webp|302]]

> [!info] Short Wavelength
> Since short wavelengths have low penetration, carriers are generated only in the P region but, due to the high recombination there, we have low probability to actually detect them.

> [!warning] Around 1 µm the energy of photons becomes lower than the energy gap of silicon.

## Electrical Model

![[Light Sensor-1791982071614.webp|392]]

- $I_{ph}$ = photo-generated current
- $I_d$ = current of the ideal pn-junction (dark current) — current generated by phenomena other than light(e.g. current is generated both by light and by thermal phenomena, therefore we have noise.)
- $C_j$ = junction capacitance: $$C_{j} = \frac{𝜀𝐴}{W_{d}}$$
- $R_{SH}$ = shunt resistance: slope at 0V
- $R_s$ = series resistance: $$𝑅_{s} = \frac{(𝑊_𝑠 −𝑊_𝑑)𝜌}{A}+R_{c}$$

![[Drawing 2026-10-14 16.05.50.excalidraw]]

## Noise

**Standard Deviation**:$$V_{n}=\sqrt{ S_{v}*\Delta f},\ I_{n}=\sqrt{ S_{i}*\Delta f}$$

### Thermal (Johnson) Noise

 In Resistors we have a so called Brownian motion of charges caused by the temperature, therefore in reality it can be modelled as the series of an ideal Voltage generator and an ideal Resistor(or with it's Norton equivalent).

![[Light Sensor-1791987413186.webp|356x215]] ![[Drawing 2026-10-15 12.50.50.excalidraw]]

**Spectral density**:$$S_{v}=4KT*R\ \left[ \frac{V^2}{Hz} \right]\\ S_{i}=\frac{4KT}{R}\ \left[\frac{A^2}{Hz} \right]$$
$4kT=1.66*10^{-20}\  \left[\frac{V^2}{Hz*\Omega}\right]$

### Shot Noise

Granularity of charge crossing a P-N junction.

![[Light Sensor-1791988073736.webp|144]]
$$S_{i}=2q*I$$

### Photodiode Noise

- Shot noise = statistical fluctuation in both the photocurrent and the dark current: $$I_{s} = \sqrt{2q(I_{ph}+I_{d})\Delta f}$$
- Thermal (Johnson) noise = thermal generation of the shunt resistance: $$I_{j\_R_{SH}} = \sqrt{\frac{4kT\Delta f}{R_{SH}}} \cdot \frac{R_{s}}{R_{SH}+R_{s}} \approx \sqrt{\frac{4kT\Delta f}{R_{SH}}}$$
- Thermal noise of the series resistance is negligible: $$I_{j\_R_{s}} = \sqrt{\frac{4kT\Delta f}{R_{s}}} \cdot \frac{R_{s}}{R_{SH}+R_{s}} \approx \sqrt{\frac{4kT\Delta f}{R_{SH}}}\cdot\frac{R_{s}}{R_{SH}} = I_{jnR_{SH}}\cdot\frac{R_{s}}{R_{SH}}$$

> [!tip] Noise Equivalent Power (NEP)
> Amount of incident light power on a photodetector which generates a photocurrent equal to the noise current: $$NEP = \frac{I_{n}}{R_{\lambda}}$$

## Basic Readout Circuits

- Non-inverting configuration: $V_o = I_{sens} \cdot R_L \cdot \left(1+\frac{R_f}{R}\right)$
- Transimpedance Amplifier (TIA): $V_o = I_{sens} \cdot R_f$
	- Typically, must be compensated! See TIA instability — increasing $C_c$.
- Light integration: $V_o = I_{sens} \cdot \frac{t_{int}}{C}$

## Photodiode Applications

- Camera: Light Meters, Automatic Shutter Control, Auto-focus, Photographic Flash Control
- Medical: X-ray Detection, Pulse Oximeters, Blood Particle Analyzers
- Safety Equipment: Smoke Detectors, Flame Monitors, Security Inspection Equipment, Intruder Alert / Security System
- Automotive: Twilight Detectors, Climate Control / Sunlight Detector
- Industry: Bar Code Scanners, Light Pens, Brightness Controls, Rotary Encoders, Position Sensors, Surveying Instruments, Copiers / Density of Toner
- Communications: Fiber Optic Links, Optical Communications

## IR Receiver Module: TSOP58238

Application circuit with Automatic Gain Control. Carrier frequency = 38 kHz.

- Peak sensitivity at 950 nm → Near Infra-Red (NIR)
- Sensitivity for light beam with 0° angle is the highest
- Same sensitivity for a light beam with 80° angle is obtained at 0.35 relative distance (relative transmission distance vs relative spectral sensitivity)

# 2. Avalanche Photodiode (APD)

- P-i-N junction
- Bias: below but close to breakdown
- Analog output proportional to incoming light
- Internal multiplication (gain: $M \approx 100$)

Basic structure:
- Absorption region = separates photo-generated holes and electrons
- Multiplication region = high electric field to provide internal photo-current gain by impact ionization

# 3. Single-Photon Avalanche-Diode (SPAD)

- P-N junction
- Bias: well ABOVE breakdown ($V_a = V_{BD}+V_{EX}$)
- Digital output
- Single photon sensitivity
- No read-out noise
- Output: >10 mA current for every photon

Solid-state device: can be integrated together with electronics (counter & TDC). Cycle: photon ($h\nu$) → avalanche → quench → reset.

# 4. Silicon Photo-Multipliers (SiPM)

Hundreds / thousands of SPADs + quenching resistance in parallel:
- Single photon sensitivity
- Photon number resolving capability
- Large active area

# 5. Light Dependent Resistor (LDR)

In the dark → $R$ very high (up to $1 M\Omega$). Exposed to light → $R$ drops dramatically (few ohms).

Working principle: photons excite electrons from the valence band to the conduction band → more free electrons in the material → lower resistance.

But:
- low sensitivity
- non-linear characteristic
- sensitive to temperature changes

Typical application (BJT switch driving LED):
- WITHOUT LIGHT: High LDR, $V_{BE} < 0.7$ → BJT off → LED off
- WITH LIGHT: Low LDR, $V_{BE} = 0.7$ → BJT on → LED on
